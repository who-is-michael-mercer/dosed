## Purpose

This policy defines how sources are selected, how community reports are treated, and how inaccurate or potentially unsafe content is corrected or withdrawn.

## Approved source hierarchy

### Tier 1 — Preferred authoritative sources

Preferred sources include:

1. Government and public-health agencies
2. Official regulatory information
3. Official medication or product labeling
4. Peer-reviewed medical literature
5. Recognized professional medical organizations
6. Established academic medical institutions

These sources should be used for safety-critical, dosing, testing, contraindication, pharmacology, and emergency claims whenever appropriate.

### Tier 2 — Supporting sources

Secondary educational or professional sources may be used to clarify technical concepts, provide general background, improve readability, and identify additional primary references. They should not replace stronger authoritative sources when stronger sources are reasonably available.

### Tier 3 — Community and anecdotal reports

Community sources include Reddit, forums, social-media posts, personal accounts, user-submitted experiences, and informal community discussions.

These sources may identify user concerns, usability issues, common questions, and topics for further research. They may not be the sole basis for dosing guidance, clinical safety claims, contraindications, diagnosis-related claims, emergency guidance, medication interaction claims, or medical recommendations. Clinically important claims discovered through a community source must be independently verified with an acceptable authoritative source before publication.

## Source recording

Safety-critical content should record, where practical:

- source title;
- organization or publisher;
- publication or revision date;
- date accessed;
- relevant URL or publication identifier; and
- content section supported.

## Routine correction workflow

1. Log the issue.
2. Michael Mercer or the designated editor evaluates the report.
3. Check the relevant source material.
4. Draft a correction.
5. Obtain clinical review if the correction affects clinical safety, dosing, pharmacology, testing, warning signs, or emergency content.
6. Commit the approved correction.
7. Record the correction in the correction log.
8. Publish the correction where appropriate.

## Urgent safety correction workflow

1. Immediately flag the affected content.
2. Notify Michael Mercer.
3. Disable, hide, or withdraw the affected feature or content when continued availability could create material risk.
4. Verify the underlying information using authoritative sources.
5. Request clinical review when clinically relevant.
6. Make the correction.
7. Independently check the correction.
8. Restore the affected content only after approval.

Urgent safety corrections take priority over the normal publication schedule.

## Withdrawal authority

Michael Mercer may temporarily remove or disable content when there is a reasonable concern that continued publication could create a material safety or accuracy risk. Clinical content withdrawn for safety reasons should not be restored until the relevant clinical review is complete.
